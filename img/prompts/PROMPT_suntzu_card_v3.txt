WHAT CHANGED, and what deliberately did not.

KEPT — the dark low-key lighting. v2 returned a near-black plate with a single

CHANGED — the armour is now CEREMONIAL rather than field, which gives the
model a reason to put polished metal on a man who would otherwise be in matt
lacquer. Three concentrations are named and located: shoulders, girdle, sword.
Naming the LOCATION matters more than asking for 'more metal', because a
specular needs somewhere specific to sit.

PERIOD NOTES — the daigou belt hook and jade sword furniture are the two great
ornamental fittings of Eastern Zhou elite dress, and gold-and-turquoise inlay
on bronze is characteristic of exactly this period. This is richer AND more
accurate than the v2 costume, not a trade.

Near black-and-white photograph with ONE exception. Everything in the frame is neutral desaturated grey — skin, cloth, leather, timber, dust — EXCEPT the metal, which keeps its full colour: warm gold and cool silver. The metal is the only colour in the picture and it must read unmistakably against the grey. Sun Tzu, author of The Art of War. Spring and Autumn period China, around 500 BC — Eastern Zhou, NOT Ming or Qing. No authentic likeness survives: render a characteristic face of the period and region rather than a portrait. A lean, spare man in his fifties, close-cropped beard, hair drawn up into a topknot under a dark cloth cap. CEREMONIAL DRESS, not field armour — the armour of a commander at court, made to be seen. Black lacquered leather lamellar, hundreds of small plates laced in rows, worn over a heavy dark silk robe. Against that near-black ground, THREE CONCENTRATIONS OF POLISHED METAL, and the light finds every one of them: ONE — THE SHOULDERS. Broad gilt-bronze shoulder plates over the lamellar, mirror-polished and slightly domed so they throw a long hard highlight along their curve, their edges chased with fine gold scrollwork. Small silver studs run the seam where plate meets lacing. TWO — THE GIRDLE. A wide belt of cast bronze plaques across his waist, each plaque polished to a mirror and inlaid with gold, fastened at the centre by a large gilded daigou belt hook — the great ornamental fitting of this period — catching the key as the single brightest thing on his body. THREE — THE SWORD, and give it the most attention of all. A straight double-edged bronze jian at his hip, NOT a curved sabre: a ceremonial blade of the highest status. Gold and turquoise inlay runs the full length of the central ridge in a fine geometric key pattern, glinting where the light crosses it. Pale carved jade pommel and jade guard. Gilt-bronze scabbard throat, mid-mount and chape, all mirror-polished, with a jade sword slide at the belt. The whole assembly reads as jewellery as much as weapon. He holds a bound bundle of bamboo writing slips against his chest in both hands, gilt characters catching the light along the strips. KEEP THE LIGHTING DARK AND LOW-KEY — this is working and must not change. A single hard source from the viewer's right, low and raking. The great majority of the frame is deep shadow and near-black lacquer; the picture is held together by a handful of small, intense, hard-edged metallic highlights that punch out of that darkness. Behind: the dark timber of a campaign tent, out of focus, fine dust suspended in the light. Camera low, looking up so the figure towers.
