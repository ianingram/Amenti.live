AMENTI · HOW TO USE IT

The hall
and the ground

Two instruments. They answer different questions and they never share the screen — that is on purpose, because they ask different things of your attention.

The hall knows people

Two thousand and forty-three souls, and for some of them a room lined with their own works — the actual texts, readable.

The map knows ground

Where a thing was, what stood on it, and why the ground mattered.

The hall

Ask Amenti.

The box at the top. Type a question and press return.

A name is not a question, and that is deliberate. Type Caesar and the hall searches — instant, and it shows you what it holds. Type what did Caesar think of Cato and it reads the room and answers. If you know what you want, ask in a sentence. If you are looking around, a word is faster.

it answers
For the building. What this place is, how it works, what a word means here, where a thing is.
it declines
What a soul thought or felt or would have done. It points you at them instead: they are standing right there, ask them. It is a doorway, not a medium.
its numbers
Were read this hour, from a register, not remembered. If a register cannot be read when you ask, it says so rather than guessing.
its limits
If nothing aboard covers your question, it tells you plainly that nothing aboard does.
A confident answer from nowhere is the one fault
this whole place was built to refuse.

An answer that rests on a text will name the work and quote from it. The hall opens at most three rooms and four passages for any question — enough to answer from the record, not so much that the answer becomes a reading list. It will also tell you what it searched and did not open, because a search that misses and says nothing is worse than one that says it missed.

The ground

Measured, not drawn.

Open it from the row of instruments at the top right. Attica, a hundred miles from the Acropolis, on real elevation data and real coastline.

And every shoreline is today’s. That is a claim, not a decoration. Thermopylae’s coast has moved six kilometres since 480 BC. The pass is in the terrain; the narrowness is not.

The marks.

A mark tells you one of three things and never blurs them.

a pin
HERE. A specific position.
a wash
SOMEWHERE IN HERE. The bounds within which to look.
nothing
Some places are held and not drawn, because the gazetteer gave a coordinate that is really the centre of a box meaning somewhere in Greece. Better nothing than a mark that lies quietly.

Nine kinds — settled, sacred, built, defence, harbour, games, worked, buried, ground — grouped by what a reader would ask, not by the gazetteer’s vocabulary.

The controls

The key, on the left.

Every row is a switch and a count. Click a row to turn that kind off.

The count is a census of what the period holds, not of what is drawn. So a switched-off row still shows its number — the register telling you what is there, while the switch tells you what is shown. When everything is off, the note says so.

Under the key is the frame list. Fifteen regions. Only Attica has been read; the rest are harvested — their places are real, their coordinates honest, and nobody has yet written down why any of it mattered. Each says so on its face.

The temporal toggle, at the bottom.

Off

The whole period — and a place gives you its whole life.

On

One year — and a place gives you the one moment alive in it.

Press play on that row and the year runs, a century either side, at six years a second. What moves is the thin layer of dated things over ground that stands still, which is roughly how a century feels from inside it.

Tours

run tour is a different button and a different clock.

It steps through a prepared sequence.

A tour adds nothing.
Every frame is a state you could reach by hand.
The story is in the order.

Which means you can stop on any frame and hover anything in it, and everything you see traces back to a file. Nothing is asserted that the registers do not already hold.

ground-first
Builds Attica up a layer at a time, from bare terrain.
marathon-490
Follows ten recorded legs — departures and arrivals, not routes.
laureion
One hillside through four periods: copper, a tyrant’s holding, the strike that paid for a fleet, and the mines stopping.

Hovering a place

A pane, bottom right.

It gives you three things, in this order.

the prose
What the place was — in that year, or across its whole life, depending on the toggle. Proper names and dates in blue.
the lines
Click to open the terse dated claims it was written from. Each carries what it rests on: corpus — the claim is in a named room of this library, and the room is named. record — general history, not checkable here, and it says so. inference — somebody joined two facts.
who names it
How many reading rooms contain a form of the name. 61 rooms means sixty-one rooms contain a form of it. It does not mean sixty-one souls wrote about the place, which is why the pane says names it.

Where a name was matched on a Latinised form, it says so: the corpus names something that transliterates to this.

The magnifier.

Hold it over the ground and it enlarges. Past ×8 the readout turns amber: the image is spent at ×8 — this is bigger pixels, not more ground.

An instrument that keeps magnifying past its source
is lying quietly.

Before you trust any of it

Four things worth knowing.

the sentences
Every why sentence and every told moment was written and not checked against a source. The registers say so of themselves and the pane repeats it. They are claims to confirm or replace, not findings.
a blank
Is not a zero. An event with no recorded duration draws no stain, because a stain of length nought would assert that the ground healed the same day.
the counts
Are read, not remembered. Ask how many souls, how many frames, how many places — it reads a register written that hour.
the frames
Fifteen exist. One is authored. That number is stated rather than smoothed over, because a frame that has not been read must not look like one that has.
