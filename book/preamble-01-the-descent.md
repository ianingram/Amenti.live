The Flying Dutchman sat perched fifty miles above the Catskills.

She had been there for hours. Anyone watching at dusk might have caught her
weighing anchor as the last of the sun went behind the ridge — a shape that was
briefly not sky — but nobody was watching, and after that there was nothing to
see. At that height she was a rumour. She held perfectly still. The thermal
dampeners held thirty degrees and gave the night nothing to find.

It was midnight.

The captain was not on the bridge. He was in the cargo hold, in a small craft: a
vehicle modified for one purpose it would perform exactly once.

He breathed deeply, lifted a mahogany pipe, and drew the warm smoke in and held
it. Then he touched a button on the console and three displays lit — dark
ground, terminal blue — and a map came up.

A subterranean map.

He went over it the way you go over a thing you already know by heart. Wind
speed. Satellite imagery. Slow cloud drifting past the anchor point and a haze
building in from the east that would be over the river inside the hour.

The time was right. Thousands of hours of research and years of planning had
come down to a window of about ninety minutes.

He knew it was down there. The probes had sent back clear images: a causeway,
hundreds of feet beneath the bed of the Hudson, laid down before the ancient of
days. Large — larger than the probe data had any comfortable way of saying.

Where it led, only he would know.

The only question had ever been direction.

---

The Hudson does not behave like a river. For a hundred and fifty miles it is a
tidal estuary — the sea comes up it twice a day, all the way past Poughkeepsie,
all the way to Troy. The Mohicans called it *the river that flows both ways* and
they were being precise, not poetic.

And it does not stop at the harbour. Off Sandy Hook the seabed opens into the
Hudson Canyon, a gorge four hundred miles long cut when the ocean stood three
hundred feet lower and the river ran out across a plain that is now abyss. The
old channel is still down there under the sediment, running east into deep
water.

So: **north, upriver toward Albany and the headwaters. Or south, downriver
through the Highlands toward the harbour, and out along the drowned canyon
toward the sea.**

He had fuel for two hundred miles in either direction and one attempt at
choosing.

He had chosen south.

---

He drew the console toward himself, opened flight control and the ordnance bay,
typed a short sequence, and waited.

Two minutes to launch.

He took a last draw from the pipe and put it back in the glove box, which is the
kind of thing a man does when he is about to fire himself into a river at a
hundred knots.

The clock counted.

At the one-minute mark a quiet alarm announced **ordnance armed**, and beneath
the vehicle two large cylinders began taking coordinates, working through their
projections with a sound like something turning over in its sleep.

And the captain's mind, which had been still for hours, began to race.

Should he abort. Was there something he had forgotten. There is always something
forgotten — that is not pessimism, it is arithmetic, across a system this size
across a plan this long. Something. He could not name it and the not-naming was
worse than a name would have been.

*Yes. That must be it.*

He hit abort.

Everything powered down.

---

The displays went dark. The cylinders stopped turning. The cargo hold went
quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when a machine that was about to
do something decides not to.

He sat.

His heart was going hard enough to feel in his throat, and under it, steady and
climbing, a beeping he had been hearing for some time without hearing it. He
had assumed it was the ordnance. It was not the ordnance.

It was his heart rate, programmed into the console of the landing craft, doing
exactly what it had been asked to do.

The beeping got louder.

He worked at it. *Relax.* The first thought is always relax, and the first
thought is usually right. He took a deep breath and it worked — the tightness
came off his chest, the beeping came down a fraction, and he reached for the
harness release, the one that would blow the door and disconnect the seatbelt
in the same motion.

It did not fire.

He was still locked into the pilot's console.

He reached for the iPad and his arms would not make the distance. Locked in, the
harness held him three inches short of it, which is nothing, which is a
lifetime.

And then he remembered what he had forgotten.

**The auto-launch sequence was the test sequence.** The one he had run forty
times in a hangar with the ordnance cold and the harness slack, the one that
aborted clean because it had never been asked to do anything else.

**The manual launch was the live one.** It was armed. Abort had powered down the
console, and the console was not what was holding the countdown.

He knew that alarm. He had written it.

It fired.

---

His heart went the way only a heart can go, and he tried — he did try, he
reached and pulled and got nothing, frantic in the way a man is frantic when the
frantic part has already stopped being useful and the body has not been told.

Twenty seconds.

At twenty seconds there is nothing to do. Touching the door mechanism at twenty
seconds would be catastrophic and he knew the number by which it would be
catastrophic, because he had calculated it himself, on a Tuesday, months ago,
in a chair, with coffee.

So he did the only thing left, which is not courage and should not be described
as courage. He removed his mind and committed to purpose.

He leaned back in the seat.

And he screamed.

---

The cargo bay door opened.

The harness went tight against his chest — properly tight, the way it is
supposed to, the way it had not been in any hangar. He got the helmet on and the
visor down with hands that were not entirely his own.

**LAUNCH** came up in green.

The Dutchman let him go.

The g-forces were enormous and exhilarating and entirely beside the point. He
fell. For one minute he fell, and the scream went somewhere useful, and somewhere
in the middle of it the entry point came up on the horizon — a bend of black
water fifty miles below and closing.

The torpedoes fired.

They left the vehicle at a speed that made the vehicle seem slow, and then the
main engines caught and the craft surged forward and struck the surface of the
Hudson at the exact instant the torpedoes touched it.

They detonated into carbon dioxide. The water went from water to foam, from
dense to almost nothing, and the landing vehicle passed through the place where
the river had briefly stopped being a river.

If anyone saw it they would have missed it.

Like a bullet entering water.

---

Entry complete.

Forty-five degrees. He had wanted forty-five degrees and he had got it, and the
craft went through the boundary and kept going, hurling itself down the angle
into water that closed over the hull and took the noise away with it.

The noise going was the strangest part. One second a scream and an engine and a
river breaking, and then nothing but the hum through the frame and his own
breathing inside the helmet.

He was free to move now. He engaged the close-range forward LIDAR and let it
paint the surfaces, and watched the returns arrive and settle and lock onto the
onboard maps.

The overlay was perfect.

Every line the probes had drawn, every contour, every bend of the old channel —
laid over the live returns and matching to within nothing. Years of it, and it
held.

He ran south with the ebb, a hundred knots, submerged.

---

The Hudson does not have one bottom. It has a floor and it has, underneath that,
the shape of something older — and about fifty miles down from where he entered
the walls begin to close and the water goes deep. The Highlands. The river cuts
a gorge through granite there and the mariners named the worst of the bend
**World's End**, because of what the currents do, and because it is two hundred
feet down where the rest of the river is thirty.

That was where he went under.

The craft began to slow and started its descent, and the walls came up on either
side on the LIDAR before they came up in the lamps.

From the rear of the cabin he heard the compressed air canisters open — a hard,
even hiss, one after another, injecting pressure into the cabin to bring it
toward the water outside. He had built that in for one reason and the reason was
that he intended to open the door at depth, and a man who opens a door at depth
without equalising first opens it once.

His ears went first. Then his chest, in a way that is not pain and is not
comfort, the sensation of being told by a machine exactly how far down you are.

It kept hissing. He kept descending.

---

The exterior lights found the rock at about a hundred and forty feet.

Bedrock. Sandstone below the granite, banded and dark and streaked with the
sediment of an unimaginable number of years — and **carved.**

Not marked. Not weathered into a shape a hopeful eye could read as a shape.
Carved: cut deliberately, at a scale that had no business being down there, the
lines running away past the reach of the lamps in both directions and holding
their proportion as they went. Monolithic and, in a way he had not expected and
did not have a word ready for, graceful. Whoever had cut it had been unhurried.

He brought the craft along it at walking pace, lamps sweeping, and the wall went
on. And on.

A river had run over this. For a length of time he could not usefully hold in his
head, a river had run over this and dropped silt on it and worn at it and given
nothing away, and every boat on the surface for four hundred years had gone over
the top of it at six knots, talking about the weather.

Long enough down there to notice that the readouts had settled, and that his
heart had settled behind them, and that the beeping had gone back to being a
sound he did not hear. Long enough to think about the abort button, and about a
Tuesday months ago, and about how a man can plan for years and still be three
inches short of the thing he needs.

Long enough that when the proximity alarm sounded he had almost stopped
expecting it.

The craft slowed to nothing.

Every calculation he had made over the past several years — every one — was
correct. The engines came down and the silt he had pushed ahead of himself
drifted past the windshield and thinned, and the lamps reached, and found.

A mass.

Square. Built. Not a formation and not a wreck — a shape with intent, resolving
out of the dark the way a building resolves out of fog.

He did not stop. He went down.

---

For several hours he descended, and the canyon changed as he went.

It got more worked. Higher up it had been carving on rock; further down it
stopped being carving on anything and became architecture, the walls losing
their geology by degrees until there was nothing left of the accident of them.
Ornate, and then very ornate, and then a kind of ornateness that stopped reading
as decoration and started reading as language he could not hold.

The lamps could not take in a whole surface. He went past things that were
probably one thing.

And then, without warning, the walls ended.

---

He came out into a flat circular floor of bedrock and the beam went wide and
found nothing to stop it.

A stadium. That was the only size he had for it. A stadium-sized disc of level
rock at the bottom of a canyon at the bottom of a river, silt lying over it soft
and undisturbed, and the water above it going up into black.

He took the controls off the computer and flew it himself.

Toward the centre, where something was.

A pyramid. Small — small the way a thing is small when the room is enormous —
dark, and squared, and set on the floor of the disc as though placed rather than
built.

He had seen it a hundred times. He had the frames on file, had gone over them at
his desk with coffee going cold, had never shown them to anybody. And it is one
thing to have the frames.

At the base, a framed doorway.

---

The craft went in quickly and silently, and there were lights.

Small ones. Landing lights, low and spaced and steady, exactly where the probes
had put them and exactly as bright, which is to say barely — enough to say *here*
and nothing else. Something had left them on.

He advanced toward a second opening, rectangular, and the lamps found the walls
of the approach: soft sand and sediment lying over ancient marble slabs, the
sand undisturbed and the marble under it holding an edge it had no right to
still hold. The whole structure was walled, and the wall was sunk into the
riverbed — not resting on it. Sunk into it, the way a thing is sunk when the bed
formed around it afterwards.

Silt turned in the lamps. Dust. And tiny shrimp — pale, hinged, entirely
unbothered — dancing in the beam in their thousands, the way they had presumably
been dancing down here the whole time.

The compressor canisters hissed on, matching him to the water.

It was very quiet.

That is the thing nobody tells you about depth. Not dark — he had lamps for dark.
**Quiet.** The kind of quiet that has been going on a long time and does not
consider you an interruption.

---

He put the craft down and went out to plant the probe.

There is no describing the walk except to say it was short and he did not enjoy
it. Soft sand over marble. Lamps behind him throwing his own shape onto a wall
that had been cut before anyone thought of the word for cutting. The water dark
and close and entirely indifferent, and the surface a hundred and forty feet
above him doing whatever the surface was doing.

He set the probe at the base of the doorway, keyed it, and watched the small
green confirm.

Then he went back, and the going back was hasty and he would not have pretended
otherwise.

Inside, hatch sealed, engines up, he looked at the display.

**Transmitting.**

---

Mission complete.

That was all it had ever been: confirm the location, plant the probe, come home.
It was not the time to open the door. It was very probably not possible to open
the door, and it was certainly not possible alone, in a modified vehicle, at a
hundred and forty feet, with fuel for two hundred miles.

These gates had not opened in a length of time he did not have a number for.
Eons is a word people use when they have stopped counting. He had stopped
counting.

He tapped the pipe against his knee out of habit, though he could not have smoked
it in a sealed cabin at pressure and knew it, and leaned back from the console,
and breathed deeply, and closed his eyes.

*Thank you.*

That was the entire thought. He did not aim it at anything.

---

Then the door took him.

It was not violent at first. It was a draw — a simple indraught, water going
somewhere the way water does, the craft shifting on its axis by a few degrees
and the readouts moving in a direction they should not have moved.

He had eleven feet of clearance in a twenty-foot opening and no time at all.

And then it *was* violent. The indraught became a throat and the craft was in it,
and the lamps showed nothing but wall going past at a speed the numbers refused
to agree on, and the pyramid and the disc and the marble were gone behind him.

**This was where every probe had stopped transmitting.**

He understood that now with total clarity and no ability whatsoever to act on
it. They had not failed. They had not been destroyed. They had been *taken*, one
after another, each one reporting perfectly right up until the moment the river
underneath the river reached out and picked it up.

His small craft was caught and thrown, at terrific speed, into a rushing body of
water running deep beneath the Hudson.

Somewhere behind him a doorway. Somewhere ahead of him a causeway, and at the end
of it a gate.

He had not opened the door.

**The door had opened him.**

---

About twenty seconds later the cockpit went off.

Every alarm at once — not a sequence, not a priority order, the whole board
lighting and calling together in a way he had never heard and had never built,
because he had never written a case in which all of them were true at the same
time.

And the canisters reversed.

They had been injecting for an hour, patiently, hiss after hiss, bringing him
down to match the river. Now they purged — a roar out of the rear of the cabin,
pressure dumping, his ears going the other way so hard and so fast that his
vision narrowed at the edges and stayed narrowed.

Which meant only one thing, and the machine had worked it out before he had.

**The water was ending.**

The craft came out of the throat and there was nothing under it.

It was ejected — thrown clear, the river spitting it into air — and for a
moment he was not submerged, was not falling, was simply going, sideways and
very fast, in the dark, with the lamps finding roof.

A cavern. Wide. He got no dimension from the lamps in the time available and no
dimension at all from what came back, because what came back was almost
nothing, which is its own kind of measurement.

The craft slammed down, skipped, and caught.

A lake. Shallow, and a soft gravel bottom under it, and the hull came to rest in
about four feet of still water with the gravel giving under it and holding.

Luck. Almost certainly luck. He would think about that later and not arrive
anywhere.

The skid had carried him a long way from where the river let go of him. He sat
for a while without doing anything at all, and then he turned the lamps out to
their long throw and looked back.

A thousand feet away, up in the dark, the water was still coming.

It came out of the bedrock overhead in a single unbroken column — the river he
had been inside a minute ago, arriving out of solid rock with no more ceremony
than a tap — and it fell past the level he was floating at and kept going, down
past the edge of the lake, down past anything the lamps could follow, into a
depth of the earth the beam simply stopped short of.

It had been doing that the entire time. While he was on the surface making
plans. While he was fifty miles up holding station. While every probe he had ever
sent went quiet, one at a time, and he sat at a desk and wondered why.

It did not make a sound he could put a size to. Just weight, arriving.

---

The captain knew where he was.

The probes had not. Every one of them had gone into the throat and come out
here, presumably, and been carried past this lake and over that edge and down
into whatever the beam did not reach — and none of them had ever known, because
none of them had been built to survive the arrival, only to report up until it.

He knew because he had been thinking about this cavern for two years without
being able to prove it existed.

He ran the checks in the order he had written them, which was the order he
always ran them in and the reason he had written them down.

**Hull integrity.** Green. The skid had cost him paint and one sensor housing
and nothing that mattered.

And the LIDAR had never stopped.

That was the thing. Through the throat, through the ejection, through the skid
across the gravel — the scanner had gone on painting returns the entire time,
because he had wired it to a circuit that had no opinion about whether anyone
was alive to read it.

Thirty-one minutes of continuous mapping of a place no instrument had ever
mapped.

He uploaded it.

The link came up on the cavern's own strange terms and the repo took the run,
and the moment it landed the route solver had it — every surface, every return,
every clearance, the whole geometry of a cavern he had been inside for four
minutes.

**A route drew itself across the display.**

Not a guess. Not a heading. A line, laid over the returns, from the hull to a
large opening in the far wall — an opening the lamps could not see and the
scanner had seen the whole time.

He engaged drive mode.

The craft came up onto the water and skimmed, gravel hissing away beneath it,
the shallow lake running out flat and black in front of the lamps, and the
column of water thundering down behind him and getting smaller.

The LIDAR had shown him all of it.

He had only had to ask.

---

The mouth of the cave was large.

He was closing on it at a speed the readout said was considerable and his eye
said was almost nothing, which is what happens when the thing you are
approaching is big enough to hold the approach. The opening did not seem to get
nearer so much as get *more*. He watched it fail to arrive for a long minute and
thought about size.

Behind him the lake went on quietly emptying itself over the edge into the
abyss, as it had been doing since before there were people to be impressed by
it.

He set the craft down lightly at the lip of the cave. Gravel, then rock. The
hull settled and the drive came off and the lamps found perhaps forty feet of
what was in front of him.

Forty feet, out of the LIDAR's return of something else entirely.

The scanner had been reporting entrances. Numerous entrances, and tributaries
running off them, and tributaries off those — a return so busy he had assumed
noise until the geometry stayed put across successive passes.

He wanted to see it.

So he launched the photon flares.

---

They went out from the craft in a long spread, and each one struck a wall and
anchored, and lit — a spark, then a flare, then a hard steady white, one after
another going off across a space that kept accepting them.

And the complex was thrown open to him.

What he saw was astounding.

---

A hall.

A marble hall, under a river, under the rock beneath the river, at a depth he
had a number for and no longer trusted.

The columns went up. That was the first fact and for a while it was the only one
he could hold — that they went up, and up, and met the roof of the tunnel where
the roof was, which was much further away than anything in his experience had
prepared him to accept. They were not tall for columns. They were tall for
anything.

**White and black, alternating.**

Not marbled. Not veined. Whole columns of white and whole columns of black,
set in strict alternation, going away from him down the axis of the hall in a
rhythm that did not falter — white, black, white, black — smaller and smaller,
until the flares stopped reaching and the pairs went on into a dark the light
had not been asked to cross.

He could not see the end. The scanner had not found one either.

---

And then he understood what he was looking at, and it was worse than
architecture.

They were not decoration. They were not for the eye and they were not for
whoever might once have walked between them.

**They were holding it up.**

All of it. Not the roof of a tunnel — the tunnel had no roof in any sense that
mattered, only a place where the marble stopped and the rock began and kept on
going. Above that, more rock. Above that, the bed of the Hudson. Above that, the
river, and the Highlands, and the granite of the whole valley, and the weight of
a landscape that had been sitting there since before there was a river to sit
under.

The columns were carrying it.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to and stopped before it finished. There
is a load figure for that much rock over that much span and it is not a number a
person holds; it is a number you write down and then look at. And it was being
held, at this moment, by white and black marble in strict alternation, going
away from him into the dark.

**Still holding. Right now. With nobody here.**

That was the emptiness of the place, and it was not a mood. It was a fact about
the load. These things had been at full working stress for a length of time that
made the word *eons* useless, unattended, uninspected, unmaintained, with the
river running over the top of them and the whole valley resting on their
shoulders — and not one of them had failed, and nothing had ever come down here
to check.

He had spent four thousand hours building instruments so that nothing in his own
system could work unwatched.

Here were the pillars of the earth, doing it perfectly, for eons, in the dark.

The flares burned on the walls, hard and white and entirely out of period, and
the black columns took the light and returned almost none of it, and the white
columns took it and held it, and the hall went away from him in strict
alternation into a distance nothing had crossed in eons.

Somewhere far above, a barge was crossing the river at six knots.

He sat in the cockpit at the mouth of it with the drive off and his hands not
yet on the controls.

*This* was the causeway.

---

Then he went in, as captains do.

The craft moved between the first pair at a pace he kept deliberately slow, and
the flares behind him made his own shadow run ahead down the floor, and the hall
took him.

And the deeper he went the more he had to bear, because the columns did not
stay plain.

They began to be worked. A band at the base of one, then a band and a course
above it, then a whole shaft carved from footing to a height the flares had to
strain for. Gold — certainly gold, there was no arguing with it, gold in the
recesses and gold following the line of the cutting. Silver, obviously, and more
of it than gold, and older-looking in the way silver goes.

And platinum. Platinum carvings, at heights nobody could have reached from any
scaffold he could imagine, set into stone a hundred feet above the cavern floor
and cut as carefully as the work at knee height.

Then the veins.

Gold, running out of the columns and into the rock — not applied, not inlaid.
**Running.** Leaving the worked stone and continuing into the raw bedrock in
lines that thinned and branched and went on, following something in the rock or
being followed by it, creeping away into the earth in every direction the lamps
turned.

He could not tell, and would never be able to tell, whether the builders had
found the gold there and built to it, or put it there and let it go.

---

How far had he travelled.

He looked at the readout and the readout answered and the answer did not help.
The number was fine. The number was just a number of a place with no other
places in it.

When would he stop.

That was the harder one, and he did not have an answer for it either, because
nothing about the hall suggested an ending and everything about it suggested he
should keep going, and he had fuel, and he had lights, and he had a route the
scanner kept extending as fast as he consumed it.

And then a thought occurred to him.

He stopped the vehicle.

He sat with it for a moment. Then he reached over and unsealed the door, and it
opened with a hiss — the pressure in the cabin finding the pressure of the hall
and finding it the same, which he had not let himself think about until that
second.

The hiss went out into the chamber and came back.

**It came back.**

It went away down the alternation of black and white, and it touched something,
and it returned to him thinned and changed and unmistakably his own, and then it
did it again from further off, and again, until it was too small to separate
from the sound of his own breathing.

Air. Breathable enough that his own lungs had already made the decision for him
before his head caught up.

Under a river. Under the rock beneath the river. Under a landscape held up by
columns nobody had checked in eons.

Air, and an echo, and it went a very long way.

---

He got out.

The exterior lights of the landing craft were the only comfort in the place and
he was aware, stepping down onto the floor of the hall, of exactly how much
comfort they were: a bright patch, and then a falling-off, and then nothing.

Cool air came in past him. Moving air — not the still air of a sealed room but
air with somewhere to be, coming down the hall from ahead of him and going out
behind. Far off, so far off it was more a pressure than a sound, water was
thundering.

The ground was dry.

That stopped him for a second. Dry stone, under a river, under the bedrock
beneath the river, somewhere beneath the Hudson Valley — dry, and swept, or at
least not silted, and firm underfoot.

Behind him, from the open cockpit, the heartbeat alarm was audible. It had been
running the whole time. Out here in the open it carried, steady and small and
unhurried, and it was the only thing in the hall that was about him.

He walked out towards the darkness.

His shadow went up the columns and onto the bedrock overhead, enormous, moving
when he moved, and he understood without needing to test it that it was the
largest thing about him down here by a very long way.

---

A vague notion began to creep over him, out in the dark, away from his vehicle.

The feeling of a traveller who has wandered too far from the main road. Not
fear, not yet — the earlier, quieter thing that comes before it and gets
ignored: an accounting. How far the lights are. How fast you can cover that
ground. Whether you would hear it if something changed behind you.

And then — what is it about sound. That you always seem to hear it the moment
before it arrives, and never in time.

A hiss.

And a flash.

Then another, closer to his person. Danger close. The flash was blinding, and
in a place where his eyes had spent an hour learning the dark it did not just
blind him, it took the dark away and gave him nothing to put in its place.

The captain began to move — and moved too quickly, which was the error, and the
error was compounded because it was dark and because whatever his eyes had known
about this floor a second ago had just been burned out of them.

Another sound, very close now.

He stumbled.

And just before he fell, a flash.

---

To fall is one thing. To fall in the dark is quite another.

He came to lying down. On his back, or on his side, or on his face — he did not
know which, and the not knowing lasted longer than it should have. His head hurt.
His shoulder hurt in a different way, a way that was going to matter later.

Some distance off there were lights.

And a buzzing. Insects, was his first thought, and enormous ones, the sound of
something with wings working hard and working close together in numbers.

Groggy. Disoriented. He got his eyes to hold on the lights and they resolved,
with the terrible plausibility of things seen from the floor, into a campfire.
He was very pleased about the campfire for slightly under two seconds.

He got to his feet, which he was lucky to have, and was not immediately certain
he still had.

Then came the few seconds when you realise you are all right — that specific,
unrepeatable relief — and they came and they went.

Because it was not a campfire.

He had come round nearer to the landing craft than he had fallen, or had crawled,
or had been moved. Its lights were still on. That was the fire. And the buzzing
was not distant at all; the buzzing was furious and frenzied and it was **at**
the craft, working over it, going round it.

The light off the hull picked out his own position for him and picked out his
sleeve, and there was blood on it, and on his hand, and he had not noticed
either.

---

And then, all at once, with a rush, they came down on him.

Like locust. That was the only shape his mind would give it — a descending mass
arriving as a single event rather than as individuals, ferocious and wild, and
each one of them lit.

**Scanners.** Fans of pale light sweeping across him, over his face and down his
body and back, reading him.

**Lasers** — thin, exact, marking points on him he could feel as heat and could
not see the purpose of.

And the wind. The beating of them, a hundred small ferocious downwashes at once,
pushing the dust off the floor of a hall where the dust had been lying still
since before there was anyone to disturb it.

More than startled. Beyond overwhelmed.

He screamed as loud as he could — the second time that day, and he would think
about that later too — and leaped forward.

At the same moment, they rushed towards him.

---

He lurched, half-blind, still reeling from the fall, one arm up across his face
— and in the middle of that motion, with a scanner sweeping past his eyes at a
distance of perhaps eight inches, he saw the housing.

He saw the housing, and he knew the housing, because he had cut the drawing for
it.

The captain stopped moving.

They went on around him, furious, frenzied, lit — and he stood in the middle of
it with his arm still up and his heart going and did the only thing there was
left to do, which was laugh.

**They were his.**

Every one of them. Every probe that had gone into that doorway and stopped
transmitting, one after another, over two years — the ones he had written off,
the ones he had costed and replaced and quietly stopped mentioning. They had not
failed. They had not been destroyed. They had come down the throat exactly as he
had, and been thrown out into the cavern exactly as he had, and then they had
done the only thing they were ever built to do.

They had carried on working.

Nobody had told them to stop. Nobody could. The signal that would have called
them home had never reached this deep, and so for two years, in the dark, under
a river, they had gone on scanning a hall that nobody had ever asked them to
scan, storing everything, and waiting for something to report to.

He put his arm down and said a word.

Just one — plain, and worn out from saying it at a desk for two years, and it
had never once been answered before.

The swarm broke apart and went up.

All of them at once, up into the space between the columns, buzzing about the
cavern in a way that had nothing efficient about it at all, lights going, and
the noise of it filling a hall that had been quiet since before the word *quiet*
had anyone to be spoken by.

---

He walked back to the landing craft with his shoulder complaining and blood
drying on his hand, and got in, and sat down at the console.

He opened the glove box and took out the pipe.

He did not light it. He held it, which was the point of it and always had been,
and he typed a short sequence into the console and watched the link come up.

Then he said it out loud, in an empty cabin, under a river, a hundred and forty
feet down.

*"All right. Tell me what you've learned."*

---

They came down out of the dark one at a time.

Each one found the hull, turned, and docked — a small definite clunk through the
frame, and then another, and another, until the craft was wearing them, and the
buzzing stopped, and the hall went back to being quiet.

The console filled with rows.

**Downloading.**

Two years of continuous observation of a place no instrument had ever mapped,
arriving into the main repo, one probe at a time, in the order they had been
lost.

---

The captain sat and smoked his pipe beneath the Hudson River and read text
files.

That is what it came to, in the end. A man in a chair, in a cabin, under a
river, under the rock beneath the river, in a marble hall held up by columns
that had been at full working stress for eons — going through plain text output,
line by line, the way he had gone through plain text output on a Tuesday
afternoon at a desk with the window open.

He had found what he came for.

More than that: he had found the thing he had not known to look for, which is
the only kind of finding worth the trip. The causeway was real. The doorway was
real. The hall went somewhere. And at the end of it, at a distance the probes had
measured and he had not yet travelled, was a gate.

The data kept arriving.

There was work to be done, and it was not the kind of work you do at a hundred
and forty feet with a bad shoulder and one craft. He would have to come back.
Properly, and with everything.

But not tonight.

---

For now he used what the probes had given him and plotted a course out.

The nav computer took the fresh returns and did in about four seconds what two
years of guessing had failed to do — it drew a way back. Not the way he had come
in. **The way he had come in was one-directional and he had the readings to prove
it now.** The throat took things and did not give them back, which was why he had
lost two years of probes to it and why he was going to lose the craft if he tried
it.

But the probes had not spent those two years sitting still.

They had been through the cave system. All of it, or enough of it — down past the
lake, over the edge, into the depth the lamps could not follow, and out. They had
found the ways through and they had never had anyone to tell.

The route went **down.**

---

He took it.

Off the floor of the hall, back between the columns — white and black and white
and black — out through the mouth, across the shallow lake with its soft gravel,
and then over the edge with the cascade, down into the part of the world the beam
had stopped short of.

It was not a fall. It was a route, and the difference is everything: a line laid
over returns, with clearances, taken at a speed the solver had chosen.

Down, and then along, and then a long slow climb through passages that had been
carrying water in the dark since before the valley above them had a shape — the
craft threading them at walking pace with the lamps finding wet rock a few feet
off either flank, and the compressors working back the other way as the pressure
came off, degree by degree, giving him his ears back.

And somewhere in there the water changed. Cold, and busy with silt, and moving
with purpose.

**The Hudson.** The ordinary one.

---

He had a thought on the way up and it stayed with him for a long time
afterwards.

That doorway had been small.

Twenty feet, in a wall, at the base of a pyramid, at the bottom of one gorge in
one river on one continent — and it had opened onto a hall he could not see the
end of, and passages the probes had spent two years failing to exhaust, and a
network of tunnels and cable running inside the rock in directions his charts had
no way to hold.

It was not a chamber. It was not a site.

**It was an access point.** One of them. A service door on something that went
under everything, and he had spent two years and every probe he owned learning
where a single handle was.

---

He came up out of the Hudson into a dark morning.

The sky was grey from one edge to the other and rain was coming down hard onto
the surface, tearing it, so that the water he broke through was already broken.

Nobody saw the craft leave the river. There was nobody to see it, and the weather
would have taken care of anyone who was — a shape coming up out of a grey river
into grey air on a morning when the whole valley had its head down.

It cleared the surface, held for a moment, and accelerated skyward.

---

The Flying Dutchman was still on station, fifty miles up, where he had left her.

He climbed to her through the weather and out the top of it, and the cargo bay
took him, and the doors closed, and the thermal dampeners went back to holding
thirty degrees over nothing anyone could see.

He uploaded the probe data into the ship's archives.

All of it — two years of it, arriving on the Dutchman in full, indexed and
stored and backed against loss, because a finding that exists in one place is a
finding you can lose in one accident.

Then he pulled up the hall.

The whole of it: the causeway, the columns, the veins in the bedrock, the long
strict alternation running away into a dark the probes had gone into and mapped
and come back from.

And at the end of it, rendered from returns rather than from hope, a gate.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he began to plan the assault on the gates of Amenti.
